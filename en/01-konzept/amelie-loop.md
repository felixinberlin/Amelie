# Amélie — The Loop

How "I have an idea" turns into a practice that can repeat without eating your year.

---

## A correction up front: "win-win" isn't quite right

The phrase is close, but it sets up the wrong expectation, and that expectation decides whether this project feels successful or like a failure after six months.

**What actually happens:** the recipient didn't ask. To them the tin is a **free option** — three minutes of reading cost, and they can let it sit with zero price. That's not a win they went looking for. Which is exactly why it's clean: nobody loses anything, nobody owes you anything, nobody has to reply.

**Your win doesn't happen at build time, it happens at delivery time** — and it's guaranteed, independent of any reply:

1. **Precision.** Forcing an idea into five parts kills the weak ones. Half your notions don't survive the "where it breaks" section. That's the work, not the loss.
2. **A map.** After a dozen tins you know who in Berlin and Europe is working on what, who has money, and when which door opens. That knowledge has value independent of Amélie.
3. **Contacts without debt.** A person at an organization knows your name from a context where you wanted nothing. That's a rare and surprisingly durable kind of acquaintance. It's the only part that compounds.

If an idea actually gets built, that's the jackpot — not the business model. Build the practice so it already pays off at points 1 through 3, and the rest is a bonus.

---

## A second correction: investors are the wrong door

Your original list included "makers, investors, researchers." Two of those are right.

Investors **don't build.** Worse: on this particular thing they're your competition, not your recipient — YC publishes its own idea lists via Requests for Startups. They have ideas in abundance and are looking for founders. Sending an idea to an investor is like bringing water to someone who lives by the river.

The only conceivable variant would be "do you know anyone building in this space?" — but that's a **favor to ask**, and as such it's no longer a gift, it's networking with an extra step. Leave investors out of the loop. If they find the public repo, fine.

**Recipient ranking, by expected hit rate** (derived from the landscape research):

| Rank | Type | Why | Code needed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Organizations with a build mandate and budget** | Have developers *and* the mandate to solve exactly this problem (co2online, CityLAB) | no |
| 2 | **Research groups** | Looking for publishable topics; a good question is currency there | no |
| 3 | **Funding bodies** | Indirectly: you give the organization the idea *and* the fund to pay for it | no |
| 4 | **Associations & communities** | Slow, but loyal and durable (SSDeV, OSM Berlin) | helps a lot |
| 5 | **Companies with a matching product** | Reply politely, rarely build — roadmaps are full | no |
| 6 | **Individual makers** | Only with a working skeleton, otherwise it's work, not a gift | **yes** |
| — | ~~Investors~~ | see above | — |

## The loop, six steps

**0 · Find** — happens anyway, costs nothing.
Ideas are not the bottleneck. You have nineteen and find new ones monthly. Resist the urge to collect more: it's the most comfortable form of procrastination, because it feels like work.

**0.5 · Check** — does this already exist? ~1 hour. **New, and learned the hard way.**
Search first, write second. Of nineteen ideas, four already existed and five turned out narrower than expected — invisible without a search. An hour here saves an hour of writing and a burned first contact. The result goes into the tin, section *Who's already tried it*; if the idea fully exists already, the loop ends here and the idea gets deleted.

**1 · Pack** — the tin, one page, ~1 hour.
Problem, why now, sketch, first step, where it breaks, who's already tried it, as-of date. If you can't fill in "where it breaks," you haven't understood the idea yet — back to step 0, or drop it.

**2 · Match** — the actual work, ~2 hours.
Who's really working on this problem? What did they publish most recently? Which person there, not which company? Is there a deadline running right now that fits them? **This is where the value of the gift is created, not in step 1.**

> **The central budget rule: one hour per idea, one per check, two per recipient.**
> This runs against every intuition, and it's the only reason Amélie doesn't end up as one more idea bank.

**Mind the time window.** For developer tooling in an active ecosystem, the lead time is **months** — check and deliver immediately there, or drop it immediately. For civil-society ideas it's years, and care pays off. The breakdown is in `dosen/_entsorgt.md`.

**3 · Deliver** — one email, one addressee, 15 minutes.
Template in `amelie-vorlagen.md`. Your name under it, CC0 over it, explicitly "you don't have to reply." For patent-exposed ideas, defensive publication first.

**4 · Let go** — zero minutes, and the hardest step.
No following up. Status to `delivered`, date entered, done. Whatever happens, happens without you.

**5 · Discard** — once a quarter, 30 minutes.
Go through every tin whose "recheck by" date has arrived: does "why now" still hold? No → delete. Don't archive. The dead idea banks of the last forty years are graveyards because nobody ever threw anything away.

## Cadence: rounds, not continuous operation

Three hours per tin is an honest estimate. Twelve tins is thirty-six hours — a work week. Fine once. Not fine monthly.

**So: delivery rounds.** Four to six tins per quarter, bundled around deadlines that are on the calendar anyway.

| Quarter | Occasion | Round |
|---|---|---|
| Q4 2026 | Prototype Fund opens Oct 1 | Altbau Thermal, Kiez-Lärmkarte, Sperrmüll-Radar + CityLAB bundle |
| Q1 2027 | FOSDEM, CCC follow-up | Dev-tooling tins, Pin Tumbler |
| Q2 2027 | CityLAB summer conference | Berlin leftovers, review of the first round |

In between: do nothing. That's part of the plan, not a lapse.

## What counts as a win

So that after six months you can judge whether this works instead of guessing:

| Event | Value | Frequency, realistically |
|---|---|---|
| **Delivered** | +1, always. Precision and the map are banked | every tin |
| **Substantial reply** | the real signal — targeting was right | 2–4 of 12 |
| **Forwarded internally** | best intermediate signal there is | rare, very good |
| **Included in an application** | almost as good as built | 0–1 per year |
| **Built** | jackpot | 0–1 in two years |

**The honest expectation for the first twelve months:** roughly twelve tins delivered, two to four real replies, in the best case one idea that lands in an application or a prototype. Anyone expecting more will mistake a working project for a failed one.

**The diagnostic criterion:** zero substantial replies after twelve tins does **not** mean the ideas are bad. It means the targeting is wrong. The response is to switch recipient type (rank 1–3 instead of 5), not to write more ideas.

## Ending it

F.A.T. Lab was loud and productive for eight years and then deliberately stopped. That's a model, not a failure.

**Amélie ends** if after a year there are neither replies nor energy left. Then: publish the archive, write a short piece on what was learned, freeze the repo. A project allowed to end is a project you can start.

**Amélie also ends immediately** if Rule 5 tips over — if you notice you're packing tins instead of building Wet Ink. Giving things away feels productive. It still isn't your work.
