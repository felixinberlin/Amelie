# Amélie — The Landscape

Who else gives ideas away, worldwide, and what became of it? As of September 2026.

The question behind this research is uncomfortable, and that's exactly why it's the right one: **if giving ideas away works, why isn't everything already built?** These projects have existed for forty years. Most of them are museums.

---

## A. The investors' wish lists

*Ideas plus money plus status. The most powerful model — and the one you can't copy.*

| Project | Country | What | State |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Y Combinator, Requests for Startups** | US | YC publishes a list per batch of ideas it wants to fund; updated continuously through 2026 (spring: 7 topics, summer: 15) | very active, the most-copied format in the world |
| **Emergent Ventures** (Mercatus, Tyler Cowen) | US | Small, fast grants to individuals with unusual ideas, $1k–50k; no application bureaucracy | active, including an AI fellowship |
| **ACX Grants** (Scott Alexander) | US | A blogger distributes private and donated money to readers' ideas, results documented publicly | runs in rounds |
| **a16z, Founders Fund and other RFS clones** | US | Topic lists as a deal-flow magnet | active, largely interchangeable in content |

**What Amélie learns from this:** this works not because of idea quality, but because money and status are attached. At YC the idea is just the bait. You have neither money nor an audience — this model is **not available** to you, and any attempt to imitate it (post a list, wait) ends up in Family B.

## B. The idea banks — the graveyard

*Ideas with no recipient. The oldest and least successful family.*

| Project | Country | Since | State |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Global Ideas Bank** | UK | 1985 as an inventors' group, online in 1995 | historical, effectively an archive |
| **Halfbakery** | international | late 1990s | still running, but a museum with cult status |
| **Idea a Day** | UK | 2000 | one idea per day, book in 2003, then stalled |
| **Ecuador Idea Bank** | Ecuador | state-run, higher-education authority | exists |
| **National Idea Bank** | Pakistan | 2021 | state-run, on the IdeaGist platform |
| **IdeasAI** (Pieter Levels) | NL | 2018– | GPT-generated startup ideas, free, upvotes |
| **r/SomebodyMakeThis** + **somebodymakethis.org** | international | Reddit | community posts wishes, a website curates them out |
| **Betterific** | US | — | consumer product-improvement wishes |

**What Amélie learns from this:** this is the family this project slides into without discipline. Forty years of idea banks, and the build rate is near zero. The reason is always the same: **an idea with no addressee is just text.** Nobody feels responsible, nobody is responsible.

Notably: `somebodymakethis.org` is already the curated version of r/SomebodyMakeThis — someone noticed the raw list was useless and layered curation on top. But curation is still just broadcasting. The next step — delivery to a named person — is the one nobody takes.

## C. The developer boards

*Ideas with an execution context. Works noticeably better, because the pool of recipients is defined.*

| Project | What | State |
|---|---|---|
| **open-source-ideas** (GitHub org + forks) | "Ever had an idea for an OSS project but no time? Let someone else try." Issues as ideas | exists, moderate activity |
| **up-for-grabs.net** | Curates projects with beginner-friendly issues | active, a standard for years |
| **florinpop17/app-ideas** | Hundreds of practice app ideas by difficulty | very popular, learning context |
| **Good First Issue / CodeTriage / First Timers Only** | same mechanism, different interface | active |
| **Wikimedia Community Wishlist** | community wishes, foundation builds; switched in 2024/25 from an annual survey to rolling submissions | active, institutionalized |

**What Amélie learns from this:** the difference from Family B is that here **someone exists whose role is to build it** — a contributor looking for a project, a foundation with a dev team. The idea doesn't land in a vacuum. Translated: delivery means finding someone for whom building is already part of the job.

The Wikimedia wishlist is the most interesting piece: it was rebuilt because the old form — collect wishes once a year, build the top 10 — left most wishes to rot. Same lesson, learned institutionally.

## D. Funders with a wish list — your real lever

*They don't give away ideas, they give away money and permission. For you they're the target, not the model.*

| Program | Country | What | As of 9/2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prototype Fund** (OKF) | DE | Public-interest tech, open-source mandatory, 6 or 10 months | **Applications from Oct 1, 2026** |
| **Sovereign Tech Agency / Fund** | DE | Funds critical open infrastructure, now also standards | active, Berlin |
| **NLnet / NGI Zero** | NL/EU | Largest European fund for open internet technology | **Open calls paused (June 2026)**, transitioning from NGI to the "Open Internet Stack" |
| **IPA MITOU** (未踏) | JP | State program funding young developers with unusual projects and pairing them with mentors; plus MITOU Junior | active, annual call |
| **UNICEF Venture Fund / DPGxYouth** | global | Funds open-source solutions, output must be a Digital Public Good | active |
| **Digital Public Goods Alliance** | global (NO/UNICEF) | Registry for digital public goods; inclusion is both a seal of approval and a distribution channel | active |
| **Awesome Foundation** | global, ~100 chapters | $1,000 with no strings, monthly, anyone can apply | active |
| **Shuttleworth Flash Grants** | ZA/global | Small amounts to individuals, no application, no accounting | a model, irregular |
| **CityLAB Berlin / GovTech TestLAB** | DE | Prototypes for public administration | active |

**What Amélie learns from this:** the most valuable package you can currently give away isn't "here's an idea," it's **"here's an idea, and here's the fund that pays for it, and it opens on October 1."** That's work the recipient organization hasn't done.

And a warning: the NLnet pause is a real loss. One of the three big European paths for exactly your kind of project is currently closed. The funding calendar in the manifest is no longer decoration — it's the most important part.

## E. The blueprint commons — the family that survived

*Idea plus substrate. The only branch that holds up over decades.*

| Project | Country | What's given away |
|---|---|---|
| **Precious Plastic** (Dave Hakkens) | NL | Blueprints for plastic recycling machines, free; rebuilt worldwide, whole workshops emerged from it |
| **RepRap** (Adrian Bowyer) | UK | Self-replicating 3D printer, open files — the origin of the entire consumer 3D-printing industry |
| **Open Source Ecology** (Marcin Jakubowski) | US | Global Village Construction Set: 50 machines for a civilization, openly documented |
| **WikiHouse** | UK | Open building systems for houses, CNC-millable |
| **L'Atelier Paysan** | FR | Farmers share tool designs, with build instructions and courses |
| **Farm Hack** | US | the same, community-driven |

**What Amélie learns from this:** this is the single most important finding of the entire research effort. These projects are 15 to 20 years old and are **alive**, while the idea banks from the same era are dead. The difference isn't money and it isn't reach, it's this: **they gave away something executable, not something merely imaginable.** A drawing, a file, a parts list.

Translated for you: the 🔨 category ("build a skeleton first, then give it away") isn't an exception for six ideas. It's the default for anything that's actually meant to get built. Pure delivery only works where the recipient already employs developers — Family D and companies.

## F. The legal mechanics — the part almost nobody in this scene uses

*How to make sure a gift can't be fenced in again.*

- **Technical Disclosure Commons (TDCommons)** — a free platform (operated by Santa Clara University School of Law) for **defensive publications**: you publish the technical description, it becomes citable, dated, and findable by patent examiners. That makes the idea **prior art** and nobody can patent it anymore. Used almost exclusively by corporations wanting to keep their own side inventions free. In the idea-giveaway scene: practically unknown.
- **CC0 / public-domain dedication** — waives all rights, as far as legally possible. The right tool for text and specifications, but **no patent protection**: CC0 doesn't stop anyone from patenting the same idea if your publication isn't findable. That's why CC0 and defensive publication complement each other.
- **Patent pledges** — Tesla opened its patent portfolio in 2014 ("All Our Patent Are Belong To You"), Volvo released Nils Bohlin's three-point seatbelt in 1959, Jonas Salk never patented the polio vaccine ("Could you patent the sun?"), and **CERN put the web software into the public domain on April 30, 1993** — arguably the most consequential gift in the history of technology.

**What Amélie learns from this:** here's a real gap you can close. For every tin where enclosure is realistic (anything hardware-adjacent, measurement and sensor ideas, processes), a defensive publication is the difference between "I gave it away" and "I made it impossible to give away." Costs nothing, takes an afternoon.

## G. The artistic lineage

*Where "the idea is the work, the execution belongs to someone else" has been practice for sixty years.*

- **Sol LeWitt, Wall Drawings** (from 1968) — he supplied certificate and instructions, execution was by others, often after his death. The work *is* the instruction. Exactly your one-pager format, just in a museum.
- **Yoko Ono, "Grapefruit"** (1964) and the Fluxus event scores — instructions as an art form, explicitly meant for execution by strangers.
- **F.A.T. Lab — Free Art and Technology** (2007–2015, including Evan Roth, James Powderly, with **Aram Bartholl in Berlin**) — a collective whose entire output was public domain. Probably the closest relative to your project: radically free, loudly signed, highly productive, with a clear ending.
- **Lewis Hyde, "The Gift" (1983)** — the theoretical foundation: a gift creates a bond, and that bond is exactly what drives a gift economy. Commodities circulate through exchange, gifts through passing-on.

**What Amélie learns from this:** see finding 5 below. Hyde contradicts one of your rules.

## H. The reversed model

*Not giving away ideas, but posting problems and collecting solutions.*

**InnoCentive / Wazoku Crowd** (US/UK), **OpenIDEO**, **Challenge.gov** (US), calls from **UNDP** and similar organizations: companies and agencies publish a problem and pay for the solution. Has run commercially for over twenty years.

**What Amélie learns from this:** the direction is reversed, but the lesson is the same: there's money for **problem + defined recipient**, not for ideas. If you ever want to fund Amélie without selling it — that's the direction someone would actually pay in.

# The seven findings

**1. Almost everyone broadcasts, nobody delivers.**
From A to H, every single project is broadcast: a list, a register, a board. The only ones who match are funding programs — and they select from applications, so they too aren't reaching out to anyone. **Nobody does person-to-person delivery after research.** Not because nobody thought of it, but because it doesn't scale. That's exactly why the niche is open.
→ Amélie is structurally small and slow. Never measure this project by volume.

**2. Idea banks rot, blueprint commons live.**
Forty years of idea banks (Family B) versus twenty years of blueprint commons (Family E): the former are museums, the latter have started industries. The difference is executability, not quality.
→ 🔨 is the default, not the exception. Bare ideas only to recipients with their own developers.

**3. Distribution beats quality — and you don't have distribution.**
YC RFS works because of YC. Isenberg's ideas work because of his audience. Your only available lever is **relevance to exactly one person**, and that lever needs no audience.
→ The repo is an archive and a URL holder, not a channel. Expect no traffic there and don't plan around any.

**4. The funding calendar matters more now, because a door has closed.**
NLnet/NGI Zero paused its open calls in June 2026. Prototype Fund opens October 1. Sovereign Tech funds maintenance, not new work.
→ The most valuable package is "idea + matching fund + deadline." Maintain the calendar like code.

**5. Anonymity is romantic and strategically wrong. — Rule 2 has to change.**
Amélie doesn't sign, and that's exactly right in the film. Hyde explains why it nonetheless hurts here: a gift creates a **bond**, and that bond is the mechanism by which gifts keep circulating. The surviving projects of Families E and G are all signed loudly — Hakkens, Bowyer, Jakubowski, F.A.T. — while releasing completely. Given away anonymously means: no second email, no callback, no referral to the next organization, no proof that it works.
→ **New Rule 2: sign it, but demand nothing.** Your name under it, CC0 over it, no attribution requirement, no reciprocity, no follow-up. Drop the anonymity, keep the unconditionality.

**6. A mechanism is missing: defensive publication.**
Nobody in this entire landscape uses TDCommons, even though it's free and solves exactly the problem a gift has — that someone fences it in.
→ New step for patent-exposed tins. Details in the manifest.

**7. Ideas have an expiration date, and the graveyards prove it.**
The dead idea banks are full of entries whose "why now" was true fifteen years ago. Your "why this works now" is a **dated claim**.
→ Every tin gets a date and a "recheck by." Whatever no longer holds gets deleted, not archived.

# Where Amélie actually stands

No project in this research combines all four properties:

1. **addressed** rather than broadcast (nobody),
2. **researched recipient** as the actual work (nobody),
3. **unconditional** in its license (Families E, F, G),
4. **dated** and actively pruned (nobody).

The closest relative is a funding program running in reverse: instead of people applying to funders with ideas, an idea applies to the people who can implement it.

Second-closest relative, and deliberately so: **F.A.T. Lab**. Small, loud, entirely public domain, productive for eight years, then deliberately ended. Aram Bartholl is based in Berlin. That's the closest model to this project — including the insight that a project like this is allowed to end.
