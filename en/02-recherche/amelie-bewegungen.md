# Amélie — The Movements

The landscape doc (`amelie-landschaft.md`) compared projects. This one goes a level deeper: **movements** built on the same idea — giving something away without getting paid for it, because it's worth more elsewhere than it is to you.

The good news up front: this is neither a niche nor a novelty. It's one of the oldest and most persistent human practices, and it has a name in every culture.

---

## I. The gift as an economic form — the anthropological root

**Potlatch** (Pacific Northwest of North America) — feasts in which rank and status were created by **giving away**, not by accumulating. Canada banned the potlatch in 1885; the ban stood until 1951.

This isn't a footnote — it's the sharpest finding of this whole research effort: **giving things away was dangerous enough to a state that it got banned for seventy years.** A gift economy isn't a nice complement to the market; it's a competing order with its own status logic.

**The kula ring** (Trobriand Islands, described by Malinowski) — shell necklaces and armbands circulate between islands, each in one direction, over years. The crucial part: **the gift moves on, it doesn't come back.** Whoever receives something eventually passes it to the next person. The bond is created by the movement, not by reciprocity.

**Marcel Mauss, "Essai sur le don" (1925)** — the three obligations of every gift economy: give, receive, reciprocate. From there the line runs straight to Lewis Hyde (1983), who turns it into a theory of the artist as a giver.

> **For Amélie:** the kula ring is the right model, not bilateral exchange. You expect nothing back — but you're allowed to expect that something **moves on**. That's the difference between "no return" and "no effect."

## II. The everyday giveaway movements

| Movement | Since | Where | What |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Freecycle** | 2003 | global | local groups where things get given away instead of thrown out |
| **Buy Nothing Project** | 2013 | global | hyperlocal, explicitly neighborhood-based, now with its own app instead of Facebook |
| **Free shops / give-away shops / Really Really Free Market** | 1990s/2000s | Europe, US | a shop with no prices; take what you need, bring what you don't |
| **foodsharing.de** | 2012 | **from Berlin**, then DACH-wide | rescued food via public "Fairteiler" fridges, rescue rather than charity |
| **Little Free Library** | 2009 | global, tens of thousands | a book cabinet in front of the house |
| **BookCrossing** | 2001 | global | release books with an ID into the world and track their path |
| **Caffè sospeso** | Naples, old | copied worldwide | pay for a coffee in advance for a stranger |
| **Karma Kitchen** (ServiceSpace) | 2007 | US/India | the bill reads zero — the guest before you paid, you pay for the one after |

Three things all these movements have in common, and all three are lessons:

1. **The gift has an object.** A book, a chair, a box of vegetables, a coffee. You can touch it, it's instantly clear what you're getting. **Ideas have no object** — which is *why* the tin has to be one: one page, recognizable format, readable in three minutes.
2. **Close beats big.** Buy Nothing deliberately stays neighborhood-sized; Repair Cafés are local; the book cabinet stands in front of *this* house. Friction is close to zero, which is why it works.
3. **Friction is the enemy.** And here Amélie is worse off than any of these movements: three hours of research plus one email is a lot of friction. **That's why Amélie is a craft, not a movement** — and why it runs in rounds instead of continuous operation.

## III. The knowledge commons — the most successful giveaway movement in history

- **Free software** — Stallman starts GNU in 1983, founds the FSF in 1985. The decisive move wasn't the appeal, it was the **license**: copyleft turns a stance into a mechanism that still works even once nobody has goodwill left.
- **Creative Commons** (2001, Lessig) — the same trick for everything that isn't code: a toolkit instead of a manifesto.
- **arXiv** (1991) — giving away preprints before publishers could lock them up. The quiet root of everything that followed.
- **Open Access** — the Budapest Open Access Initiative in 2002, then Plan S / cOAlition S. The honest twist: the strict requirements have since been walked back; the thing is now carried by **Diamond Open Access** — journals that charge neither readers nor authors, mostly run by universities and learned societies.
- **Wikipedia, Open Data, Open Hardware (OSHWA)** — same family, different materials.

> **For Amélie, and it's the single most important lesson in this document: the mechanism beats the stance.** The GPL gave away more than any manifesto ever did. Translated: it's not the manifesto that makes your project durable, it's CC0 plus defensive publication. The manifesto only explains why.
>
> And Open Access shows the flip side: when a movement gets institutionalized, it dilutes. What's left is the part that people do without money, out of conviction.

## IV. Commons against enclosure — seeds and repair

**Open Source Seed Initiative** (US, since 2012/14) and **OpenSourceSeeds** (Germany): plant varieties are kept permanently free via a **pledge** — whoever takes seed may use it, breed it, and pass it on, but may never patent or restrict it. The pledge is printed on the packet. Short, understandable, viral.

That's the direct parallel to your defensive publication: it's not about giving something away, it's about keeping it **impossible to fence in**. A gift someone can enclose was never a gift, it was a template.

**Repair Café** — started in 2009 by Martine Postma in Amsterdam, now thousands worldwide. And here's the punchline: the **EU Right to Repair directive** was adopted by the European Parliament on April 23, 2024 (584 of 705 votes); member states have two years to implement it from publication on July 10, 2024. **Repair Cafés are named explicitly three times in the legal text**, including a recommendation to give them space in community and cultural centers.

> **For Amélie:** fifteen years from the first workshop in Amsterdam to a European directive text. A volunteer movement *can* change legislation — if it keeps doing the same visible thing for long enough. That's the realistic timescale for institutional impact. Not discouraging, but honest.

## V. The big gift movements — and where they failed

**Bhoodan / Gramdan** (India, from 1951). Vinoba Bhave walks across India and **asks large landowners to give away land** — to the landless, voluntarily, with no law behind it. It works at a scale that's hard to believe: individual donations from hundreds up to 200,000 acres, the Nizam of Hyderabad gives 14,000 acres, from 1952 entire villages give themselves away (*Gramdan*), starting with Mangroth.

And then: from the 1960s the movement loses momentum. Later, much of the donated land is lost again to encroachment, because handover, registration, and follow-up were never cleanly regulated.

> **For Amélie, the most uncomfortable lesson: gifts without bookkeeping drift away.** You don't have to follow up — but someone has to record what was given to whom and when, or the gift is untraceable in ten years and was, in the end, none at all. Your status list isn't paperwork, it's the difference between Bhoodan and a rumor.

**C-TAP and the Open COVID Pledge** (2020). During the pandemic, patents and know-how for vaccines and tests were supposed to go into a shared pool. The Open COVID Pledge got commitments from tech companies; C-TAP at the WHO stayed largely empty because the key manufacturers didn't participate.

> **For Amélie:** **voluntarism doesn't scale against economic interest.** Giving things away works where nothing big is at stake — or it doesn't work at all. Don't expect an idea with real market value to land anywhere this way. The tins that will land are the ones nobody gets rich from.

## VI. The religious and philosophical traditions

- **Dāna** (Buddhism, Hinduism) — the gift as a practice for the giver, not as a transfer to the receiver. In the Buddhist formulation, the **gift of teaching** counts as the highest form: knowledge is the gift that doesn't diminish when shared.
- **Sadaqah jariyah** (Islam) — the *continuing* charitable act: giving something that keeps having an effect after you're gone. **Useful knowledge is one of the classic examples cited.** The associated institution is the *waqf*, the permanent endowment.
- **Tzedakah** (Judaism) — Maimonides ranks eight levels of giving. Anonymous giving ranks high; the **highest** level is the one that makes the recipient **self-sufficient**, rather than keeping them dependent.

> **For Amélie:** three traditions that never coordinated historically say the same thing. First: **knowledge is the gift with the longest half-life**, because it isn't consumed by sharing. Second: the best gift creates independence rather than binding. An idea does exactly that — it empowers and doesn't obligate. Money does the opposite.
>
> Incidentally, Maimonides also defuses the signing question: anonymity is *a* high level, but not the highest. The highest is the one that works.

# What these movements teach, together

**1. The gift needs an object.** A book, a box, a coffee, a seed packet. Your tin is the object. It has to be recognizable as a thing, not as a paragraph in an email.

**2. Mechanism beats appeal.** GPL, CC0, the OSSI pledge, defensive publication. Anything that relies only on goodwill lasts exactly as long as the goodwill does.

**3. Close beats big.** Every functioning everyday movement is hyperlocal. For you: Berlin recipients first, not because they're better, but because you can actually meet them.

**4. Voluntarism doesn't scale against money.** The ideas that land are the ones without much market value. That's not a weakness of the model, it's its domain.

**5. Without bookkeeping, everything drifts away.** Bhoodan gave away millions of acres and lost track. Keep the list.

**6. Institutional impact takes 10–15 years.** Repair Café, from Amsterdam to an EU directive text. If you ever want to operate at that scale: it's possible, just not this year.

**A note on Ostrom:** Elinor Ostrom's eight principles for functioning commons are the obvious reference, and they **don't fit here**. They solve the problem of overusing a finite resource — pastures, fisheries, water. Ideas are non-rival: if I give you one, I still have it. Amélie doesn't have a commons problem, it has a **distribution problem**. Exactly one Ostrom principle stays useful: *monitoring* — keep a record of what goes out. See point 5.

# What Amélie is — and what it isn't

**Not a movement.** Movements consist of many people doing the same **small** thing: paying for a coffee in advance, fixing a toaster, putting a book on a shelf. Amélie is one person doing something **large and rare**: pouring three hours of research into a gift nobody ordered. That doesn't multiply, and it doesn't need to.

**If it should ever become one,** the pattern from this research is clear: don't shout "give your ideas away!" — put a **format** and a **pledge** into the world, the way OSSI prints its formula on the seed packet. One sentence that goes on every tin and carries itself forward:

> ## The Amélie Pledge
>
> **This idea belongs to no one. Take it, build it, sell it — you owe me nothing, not even a reply. If you ever have an idea you won't build, give it to someone who will.**

That's the kula ring in three sentences: the gift moves on, it doesn't come back.

## And now the part that ends well

This whole research effort could have ended depressingly — potlatch banned, Bhoodan petering out, C-TAP empty, Plan S diluted. But it doesn't.

Giving things away is one of the few human practices that **survives without institutions**. It's been banned, and it came back. It's been institutionalized, and it resurfaced somewhere else. From the kula ring to the book cabinet on the corner, from Vinoba Bhave's marches to the seed packet with the pledge printed on it: it simply doesn't stop, because it's apparently something people like doing.

You can do it badly — spread too thin, keep no records, burn yourself out on it. But you can't break it, and nobody can forbid it to you.

And nothing in this entire history says a gift has to be big to count. A coffee counts. A single page of paper, landing with the right person, counts too.
